Why tax structure matters

Public taxation is the fundamental lever through which a state finances its public services and regulates its economic activity. For an analyst, the tax structure is not merely a simple accumulation of revenue; it reflects a nation's political choices, administrative capacity, and development priorities. By observing the ratio of tax revenue to GDP, we measure the weight of the state in the economy. At the same time, the breakdown of taxes—whether they are levied on income, consumption, or international trade—reveals the progressivity or regressivity of the system. Reading these indicators allows one to understand how a country distributes the tax burden among its citizens and economic agents. In a globalized context, comparing this data with models such as those of Morocco, France, or Germany offers valuable perspective on the budgetary room for maneuver and the structural challenges facing Argentina.

Key figures explained

Key indicators provide a precise portrait of the Argentine tax system. Tax revenue, expressed as a percentage of GDP, rose from 4.8% in 1990 to 10.4% in 2024, with a historical peak of 13.3% in 2008. This moderate tax burden highlights a fluctuating collection capacity. The composition of revenue is equally revealing: taxes on income, profits, and capital gains reached 9.1% of total revenue in 2024, far from their historical maximum of 19.8% in 2005. Taxes on goods and services, the pillars of collection, accounted for 31.8% of revenue in 2024, compared to a low of 22.0% in 1990. Finally, duties on international trade, which stood at 15.0% in 2024, illustrate a marked dependence on foreign trade, with a peak of 20.1% in 2008. These figures, while aggregate, highlight the system's reliance on indirect taxes and cross-border flows rather than direct income taxation.

Historical overview

Argentina's fiscal evolution since 1990 reveals phases of profound transformation. Between 1990 and 2000, the country experienced a gradual increase in the tax burden, rising from 4.8% to 9.6% of GDP, driven by a constant increase in taxes on goods and services. The crisis of the early 2000s marked a turning point: while total revenue fell to 9.3% in 2001, rapid restructuring allowed it to reach 12.5% by 2003. This period saw a spectacular rise in international trade duties, which jumped from 4.3% in 2001 to 13.6% in 2002, offsetting internal difficulties. The 2008 peak (13.3% of GDP) coincided with maximum dependence on customs duties (20.1%). Since 2010, the trajectory has been more erratic, with a slow erosion in the share of income taxes, which fell to 8.5% in 2023 before rising slightly to 9.1% in 2024. This historical series shows a certain resilience, but also a structural difficulty in stabilizing a diversified tax base, with the country often oscillating between the need to tax consumption and the need to capture revenue from foreign trade to balance its public accounts.

Tax revenue structure

The Argentine tax structure is based on an unbalanced triptych. Taxes on goods and services constitute the main source of income, capturing 31.8% of total revenue in 2024. This choice favors broad and immediate collection but may weigh more heavily on low-income households. Duties on international trade occupy a unique position at 15.0% of revenue, a level significantly higher than in most developed economies. This dependence on customs duties makes the state budget vulnerable to fluctuations in global prices and trade volumes. Conversely, taxes on income, profits, and capital gains, which account for only 9.1% of total revenue, occupy a relatively small place. This low share of direct taxes suggests a difficulty in capturing the wealth produced by companies and individuals in a progressive manner. In short, the Argentine system is heavily geared toward indirect taxation and the flow of goods, which technically simplifies collection but limits the capacity for redistribution through direct taxation. This configuration contrasts with more mature systems where income tax often forms the bedrock of tax justice and public revenue stability.

Strengths

  • The ability to mobilize duties on international trade offers a quick lever for revenue during periods of budgetary tension. This allows the state to capture a portion of the value of trade without relying solely on domestic activity.
  • The predominance of taxes on goods and services ensures a broad and constant collection base. This mechanism allows for the generation of tax revenue on an almost daily basis through current consumption.
  • The historical resilience of total revenue, despite economic crises, demonstrates a certain adaptability of the tax system. The administration has been able to adjust rates and tax bases to maintain a minimum level of public funding.

Weaknesses and areas for consideration

  • The low share of taxes on income and profits could be examined as a barrier to fiscal progressivity. An increased reliance on indirect taxes tends to weigh more heavily on the purchasing power of the most vulnerable households.
  • The high dependence on international trade duties exposes the national budget to external shocks and the volatility of global markets. This vulnerability could limit the predictability of public revenue in the long term.
  • The significant fluctuation in revenue as a percentage of GDP suggests potential instability in budgetary planning. A more diversified tax base could help smooth out these variations and secure public spending.

International comparison

International comparison reveals marked structural divergences. With 10.4% of tax revenue in 2024, Argentina falls into a category close to Germany (10.9%), but very far from France (22.8%) or Morocco (22.7%). This moderate tax burden places Argentina in a situation where the room for maneuver for financing public services is more restricted than in the European or Maghreb economies cited. Regarding structure, Argentina stands out for its international trade duties (15.0%), whereas France shows a rate of 0.0% and Morocco 3.8%. This Argentine specificity highlights an outward-looking fiscal approach. Regarding income taxes, Argentina (9.1%) is well below Morocco (34.7%) and France (29.1%), which indicates a lesser use of direct taxation as a tool for redistribution. Finally, the share of taxes on goods and services in Argentina (31.8%) is higher than that of France (18.9%) and Germany (20.0%), although lower than in Morocco (45.3%). This analysis shows that Argentina favors indirect and customs-based taxation, whereas its comparison partners, particularly European ones, rely more on direct taxation of income and profits to finance their social model.

Reform avenues to examine

  • Strengthen the share of taxes on income and profits to increase the progressivity of the system. This reform would reduce dependence on consumption taxes and improve fiscal equity.
  • Diversify the tax base to reduce dependence on international trade duties. Such a measure would reduce the national budget's vulnerability to exogenous shocks and commodity cycles.
  • Simplify the tax framework to encourage the broadening of the taxpayer base. More efficient administration and a reduction in tax loopholes could increase revenue without necessarily increasing nominal rates.

Limitations of these data

These indicators, while robust, have limitations. They do not reflect nominal tax rates, specific exemption systems, or the complexity of local taxes that sometimes escape consolidated national statistics. Furthermore, the significant share of the informal economy in Argentina is only partially captured by this data, which may underestimate the actual tax burden on the formal sector. Finally, these figures do not allow for an assessment of the efficiency of public spending or the quality of services provided in return for the levies.

Conclusion

Argentina has a unique tax system, strongly correlated with trade flows and consumption. While this structure has allowed for a certain resilience, it would benefit from diversification to ensure greater budgetary stability. The future challenge lies in balancing the need for immediate revenue with the construction of a more equitable and sustainable tax system.
