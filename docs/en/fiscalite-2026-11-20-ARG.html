Why tax structure matters

Taxation is the foundation upon which a state's economic sovereignty rests. Understanding a country's tax structure requires analyzing not only the overall level of revenue relative to GDP, but also the composition of these levies. The indicators selected here—taxes on income, goods and services, and taxes on international trade—allow for an assessment of a tax system's reliance on different tax bases. High tax pressure may reflect a significant capacity for redistribution, while a heavy concentration on indirect taxes can weigh on household consumption. By examining data from Argentina and comparing it with that of Morocco, France, and Germany, we highlight strategic public policy choices. This educational overview aims to make complex mechanisms accessible, which are essential for grasping the challenges of fiscal sustainability and tax equity in a globalized economic context.

Historical overview

Argentina's fiscal evolution since 1990 reveals several distinct phases. During the 1990s, tax revenues were relatively low, oscillating around 5% to 9% of GDP. The turn of the millennium marked a significant break: between 2002 and 2004, revenues surged, rising from 9.8% to 13.1%, reflecting a desire to strengthen state capacities. The 2003-2015 period was characterized by more sustained tax pressure, remaining mostly above 12% of GDP. We also observe a marked dependence on taxes on international trade, which climbed from 4.3% in 2001 to 20.1% in 2008, illustrating a strategy of capturing rent related to external trade. After 2015, a downward phase began, with revenues falling back below the 11% of GDP mark around 2017-2018. Recent years, marked by macroeconomic instability, show persistent volatility, with revenues returning to 10.4% in 2024. This historical series highlights a structural difficulty in maintaining a stable tax base over the long term, with disruptions often correlated with the major economic crises the country has undergone, forcing frequent adjustments to tax rates and bases.

Tax revenue structure

The Argentine tax structure rests on an unbalanced triptych. The predominant share of taxes on goods and services (31.8% of revenue in 2024) indicates a strong reliance on final consumption, which is a classic characteristic of systems seeking rapid and broad collection. Conversely, the share of taxes on income and profits (9.1% in 2024) remains relatively modest compared to the standards of developed economies. This suggests a tax base less focused on the progressivity of direct taxation. The third pillar, taxes on international trade (15.0% in 2024), is a notable specificity. Unlike advanced economies that have largely liberalized their trade, Argentina maintains a significant share of its revenue through these duties, acting as a direct lever on the flow of goods. This fiscal architecture reflects a trade-off between the need to collect immediate resources via consumption and trade, and the difficulty of implementing a robust and effective direct taxation system. This composition highlights a system that prioritizes the ease of collecting indirect taxes to the detriment of direct taxation, which is more complex to administer and to gain social acceptance for in a volatile economic environment.

Strengths

  • The capacity to mobilize revenue via taxes on goods and services ensures immediate liquidity for the state budget. This structure allows funds to be collected at each stage of the value chain.
  • The use of international trade duties provides a lever for regulating external flows. This allows the State to adjust its revenue based on the country's export and import activity.
  • The resilience of tax revenues, despite crises, demonstrates a certain adaptability of the system. The country has managed to maintain a minimum level of collection even in highly complex economic contexts.

Weaknesses and areas for consideration

  • Reliance on indirect taxes could be examined for its regressive impact on the purchasing power of lower-income households. Such a tax structure weighs proportionally heavier on small incomes.
  • The volatility of revenue from international trade could be an avenue to explore for stabilizing public finances. This dependency makes the budget vulnerable to fluctuations in global prices and trade volumes.
  • The relatively low share of taxes on income and profits suggests an area to examine regarding the broadening of the tax base. Strengthening direct taxation could improve the progressivity and equity of the overall system.

International comparison

Germany, at 10.9%, is closer to the Argentine level, but with a different internal structure. Conversely, for taxes on goods and services, Argentina (31.8%) is positioned between France (18.9%) and Morocco (45.3%), confirming its propensity for indirect taxation. The Argentine specificity lies in its international trade duties (15.0%), where France shows 0.0% and Morocco 3.8%. This comparison shows that Argentina, unlike European countries, retains a strong customs component in its revenue, while Morocco and France favor a more structured internal tax system. Germany, although close in terms of overall tax burden, seems to rely on a more significant direct tax base, illustrating distinct societal choices and administrative capacities in managing compulsory levies.

Reform avenues to examine

  • Rebalance the structure by strengthening income tax to improve progressivity. This reform would aim to reduce dependence on consumption taxes, thereby fostering greater social justice.
  • Modernize the tax administration to broaden the base of direct taxes. The goal is to reduce tax evasion and increase revenue without necessarily increasing existing rates.
  • Gradually reduce dependence on international trade duties to foster trade integration. Such a measure would encourage the competitiveness of local companies in global markets.
  • Simplify the system of taxes on goods and services to reduce compliance costs. This would improve collection efficiency and limit economic distortions.

Limitations of these data

These indicators, while useful, have limitations. They do not reflect nominal tax rates, tax loopholes, or the specifics of local or provincial taxes, which play a major role in Argentina. Furthermore, they do not capture the scale of the informal economy, which largely escapes these statistics. Finally, the distinction between tax revenue and social contributions is not detailed here, which prevents a complete analysis of the total cost of labor or social protection.

Conclusion

Argentine taxation is a system in search of balance, oscillating between immediate financing needs and long-term development goals. While reliance on indirect taxes and customs duties has helped maintain revenue, it also highlights room for improvement toward a more direct and equitable tax system. Future evolution will depend on the country's ability to stabilize its macroeconomic framework and modernize its collection tools.
