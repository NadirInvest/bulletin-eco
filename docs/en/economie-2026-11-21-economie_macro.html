Financial inclusion: a lever for transforming emerging economies

Financial inclusion has become a strategic pillar for reducing poverty and stimulating growth in developing countries. Thanks to the digital revolution, access to formal banking services is expanding, although structural challenges persist. This article analyzes the dynamics, technological tools, and conditions necessary to transform this access into a true engine of development.

Why this topic matters

Financial inclusion refers to the ability of individuals and businesses to access affordable and secure financial products and services that meet their needs: transactions, payments, savings, credit, and insurance. In developing countries, a large portion of the population remains "unbanked," which limits their ability to invest, protect themselves against economic shocks, or participate fully in the formal economy. This subject is crucial because access to capital is the fuel for entrepreneurship and social mobility. Historically, physical banking infrastructure was too expensive to reach rural areas or low-income populations. Today, the situation is changing thanks to mobile technologies and digital finance (fintech). However, inclusion is not limited to opening an account; it requires solid financial literacy and a protective regulatory framework. Understanding these issues allows decision-makers and managers to better grasp the levers of inclusive growth, while identifying the systemic barriers that still prevent millions of people from joining the global financial system.

Key takeaways

To understand financial inclusion, one must distinguish between three fundamental dimensions: availability, penetration, and effective usage. Availability concerns the existence of infrastructure (branches, mobile networks, banking agents). Penetration measures the rate of account ownership. Usage, often the weak link, reflects the frequency and relevance of the services used by clients. A key concept is that of the "barrier to entry": it can be voluntary (lack of trust, financial illiteracy, cultural norms) or involuntary (strict KYC/AML regulations, lack of identification documents, geographical distance). Technology, particularly mobile banking, acts as a catalyst by reducing transaction costs. However, the stability of the banking sector remains a sine qua non condition: solid banks are necessary to manage risks and maintain public trust. Finally, the emergence of sustainable finance and Islamic finance offers alternative models to capture segments of the population excluded from conventional channels, proving that inclusion is as much a matter of technology as it is of cultural and social adaptation of financial services.

What the studies say

[1] Trends and drivers of inclusion (2010-2023)

This study analyzes 41 developing countries to identify the drivers of financial access. It concludes that inclusion is essential for poverty reduction and socio-economic balance. The results highlight that the mere provision of services is not enough: active public policies, focused on financial literacy and robust regulatory frameworks, are essential to transform access into concrete economic progress. The study insists on the need to adapt institutions to the actual capabilities of local populations to ensure sustainable adoption.

[2] Fintech composite index (2011-2021)

The authors propose a new composite index measuring inclusion via digital finance in 40 countries, incorporating mobile and Internet usage. The methodology uses principal component analysis to avoid weighting biases. The study reveals a notable progression in the use of banking agents and digital tools, but highlights major obstacles: the need for massive investment, the necessity for increased financial education, and the imperative of consumer protection. The robustness of this index makes it a valuable decision-making tool for identifying specific gaps by country.

[3] Role of multinational banks

This research examines the impact of the social responsibility practices of multinational banks (MNBs) on inclusion. By analyzing 152 banks in 32 countries, the study demonstrates that the adoption of sustainable practices strengthens the trust of local populations. This climate of trust reduces perceived risks and directly stimulates three indicators: demand for bank accounts, savings, and access to credit. The study proves that MNBs are not just financial actors, but agents of social change when they integrate sustainability into their operational model.

[4] Challenges and barriers to inclusion

This literature review distinguishes between involuntary exclusion (systemic, regulatory) and voluntary exclusion (cultural, lack of trust). Although digital technology is a powerful lever, the study notes a persistent gap between theoretical access and actual usage. The authors advocate for a holistic approach: flexible regulatory reforms, investments in public digital infrastructure, and financial literacy programs. The major conclusion is that financial inclusion must be a means to serve equitable and climate-resilient development, rather than an end in itself.

[5] Banking stability and growth

Studying 35 countries between 2011 and 2022, this research analyzes the link between banking sector stability and economic growth. Using standard error correction techniques, the study confirms that banking stability is a driver of growth. Stable banks support economic activity by effectively managing risks, providing credit, and consolidating public trust. The limitation of the study lies in its focus on macro-prudential stability, without detailing the micro-economic mechanisms of inclusion for the most precarious populations.

[6] Islamic finance and Fintech

This bibliometric analysis (2018-2024) explores the role of Islamic Fintech in financial inclusion. With a marked growth in publications, particularly in Malaysia and Indonesia, the study identifies P2P lending, blockchain, and digital payments as dominant themes. Islamic finance, through its ethical principles, offers an accessible alternative to populations excluded from the conventional system. The study concludes on the need for a specific regulatory framework and increased collaboration between institutions to sustain this inclusive and durable ecosystem.

Synthesis: convergences, divergences, and uncertainties

Studies converge on a central idea: financial inclusion is a driver of development, but its effectiveness depends on the quality of usage rather than the mere availability of services. Technology (fintech, mobile banking) is unanimously recognized as the primary lever for reducing costs and reaching isolated areas. However, a divergence emerges regarding the priority of actions: some insist on the need for heavy regulatory reforms to secure the system, while others emphasize financial literacy and strengthening user trust. There remains uncertainty regarding the ability of current models to effectively protect consumers against rising digital risks. Furthermore, while the positive impact of sustainable and Islamic finance is highlighted, their ability to scale globally remains an open research topic. In short, financial inclusion cannot be treated in isolation; it requires close coordination between regulators, private institutions, and education stakeholders to transform digital tools into true vectors of shared prosperity.

Implementation: where to start

  1. Accessibility audit
    Evaluate the real barriers to entry for your target clients. Do not settle for data on the number of accounts opened; analyze abandonment rates during registration and the frequency of transactions. Identify whether the blockage is technological, linked to the complexity of required documents, or due to cultural distrust of institutions.
  2. Deployment of digital solutions
    Prioritize simplified interfaces, accessible on basic phones (USSD) and available in local languages. The goal is to reduce technological friction. Avoid transposing complex banking models; adapt features to the micro-transaction and local savings needs of target populations.
  3. Financial literacy programs
    Do not launch any product without supporting the user. Integrate simple financial education modules directly into your applications. Success depends on the client's understanding of risks, rates, and benefits. An educated client is a loyal client and less exposed to fraud.
  4. Strengthening trust
    Adopt practices of radical transparency regarding fees and terms of service. Trust is the most valuable capital in developing countries. Use networks of trusted local agents to serve as a human interface between technology and populations, thereby reassuring the most reluctant users.
  5. Flexible and ethical compliance
    Work with regulators to implement KYC (Know Your Customer) models that are proportional to risks. Avoid excessive documentation requirements that exclude informal populations. Ensure that your practices respect local ethical standards, whether they are conventional or based on principles of responsible or Islamic finance.

Indicators to track

  • Active account usage rate (transactions per month)
  • Average cost of financial transactions relative to local income
  • Percentage of the population with access to mobile financial services
  • User financial literacy index
  • Penetration rate of micro-credit and savings services

Pitfalls to avoid

  • Confusing access to services with actual and beneficial usage.
  • Neglecting the importance of financial education for users.
  • Imposing overly rigid compliance processes for small accounts.
  • Ignoring cultural barriers and the need for local trust.

For reflection within your organization

  • How can our business model integrate the ethical dimension to strengthen user trust?
  • What regulatory barriers currently prevent our services from reaching the most precarious segments?
  • How can we measure the real impact of our services on poverty reduction beyond simple profit indicators?

Conclusion

Financial inclusion is a powerful yet complex lever that requires a holistic approach combining technology, regulation, and education. To succeed, decision-makers must place the end user and their trust at the heart of every innovation. It is by transforming access into useful usage that we will build more resilient and equitable economies.
