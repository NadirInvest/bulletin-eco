Customs duties: understanding the mechanisms and real impacts on global trade

Customs duties have once again become a central lever of global economic policy, directly influencing supply chains and growth. This article analyzes the complex effects of these taxes on international trade through the latest academic research. Discover how these measures durably shape the strategies of companies and states.

Why this topic matters

In a context of heightened geopolitical tensions, customs duties are once again emerging as a major tool of protectionism. A customs duty is a tax imposed by a government on imported goods, generally aimed at protecting domestic industries from foreign competition or correcting trade imbalances. While their logic seems simple—making imported products more expensive to favor local production—their macroeconomic consequences are much broader. They influence not only consumer prices but also overall productivity, investment flows, and the stability of international relations. For decision-makers and managers, understanding these mechanisms is crucial: a tariff decision can trigger cascading effects, ranging from inflation to the disruption of global value chains. This article explores the complex dynamics between protectionism and free trade, drawing on recent research to decode how these barriers are truly altering the global economic landscape and corporate strategies.

Key takeaways

To grasp the impact of customs duties, one must look beyond the simple notion of a tax. The primary mechanism is the increase in the cost of inputs, which penalizes companies dependent on imported components. Next, there is a feedback effect: the targeted countries often retaliate with counter-measures, triggering a trade war that increases market volatility. It is also necessary to distinguish between tariff barriers (direct taxes) and non-tariff barriers, such as technical standards or quotas, which can be just as restrictive. A key concept is the "time lag": the effects of a tax are not immediate. They diffuse through the economy via inflation, which may force central banks to adjust interest rates, thereby impacting investment and consumption. Finally, international trade theory emphasizes that while tariffs may protect a specific sector in the short term, they often harm overall economic efficiency by limiting access to advanced technologies and reducing the competitiveness of national exports in the long term.

What the studies say

[1] Global impact on growth

This study analyzes the impact of tariffs on economic growth from 1990 to 2020. It demonstrates a significant negative correlation between customs duty levels and GDP growth, particularly for export-dependent economies. The authors highlight that while tariffs protect local jobs in the short term, they harm productivity, capital formation, and access to technology. The main limitation lies in the difficulty of isolating the effects of tariffs from other complex macroeconomic variables influencing global growth.

[2] Modeling adjustment delays

Using delay differential equations, this research models tariffs as a control mechanism in an economic system. It reveals that adjustment delays amplify initial impacts, causing inflationary pressures that trigger responses from central banks. The study provides a theoretical framework for anticipating dynamic consequences on consumption and investment. The limitation is the complexity of the model, which requires highly precise data to reflect the specificities of each national economy.

[3] Effects of the Korea-EU agreement

This analysis breaks down the tariff and non-tariff effects of the Korea-EU free trade agreement. Contrary to expectations, Korean exports fell by 17.6%. The study shows that while tariff reductions favored trade, non-tariff measures neutralized these gains, particularly in the manufacturing sector. The limitation of this study is that it focuses on a specific agreement, making it necessary to be cautious when generalizing the results given the diversity of international trade agreements.

[4] India-USA agricultural trade

Using a gravity model, this study assesses the impact of trade barriers on the agricultural sector between India and the United States. The results indicate that the elimination of tariffs would significantly boost American exports while reducing prices for Indian consumers. The study highlights the growth potential for products such as fresh fruits and nuts. The limitation is that the analysis focuses solely on the agricultural sector, without taking into account the repercussions on other industrial sectors.

[5] Trade wars and the long term

This research examines the long-term consequences of trade wars, such as the USA-China conflict. It identifies lasting effects such as supply chain disruption, financial volatility, and the need to diversify trade partners. The study concludes that global prosperity depends on stable policies rather than protectionist national measures. A limitation is the qualitative nature of certain conclusions regarding international relations, which are highly dependent on the changing political context.

[6] Effects of Trump 2.0 tariffs

This ex-post study analyzes monthly export data from 31 countries to the United States in 2025. It highlights an anticipation effect: a rise in exports just before the implementation of tariffs, followed by a sharp drop. Countries subsequently redirected their flows toward smaller economies. The limitation of this study is the very short observation period, which does not yet allow for measuring the definitive structural effects on global value chains.

Synthesis: convergences, divergences, and uncertainties

Studies converge on an essential point: while tariffs are designed as protectionist tools, their side effects—inflation, supply chain disruption, and retaliatory measures—often end up canceling out the expected benefits. There is a consensus that protectionism reduces productivity and long-term growth. However, divergences emerge regarding the effectiveness of free trade agreements, where non-tariff barriers (standards, regulations) can become more significant obstacles than the taxes themselves. What remains uncertain is the ability of companies to reorganize their value chains sustainably in the face of increasing political volatility. Models show that economic adjustment lags make forecasting difficult for decision-makers. In short, the research highlights that prosperity is better served by stability and cooperation than by unilateral measures, even though the latter are politically appealing in the short term. Diversifying trade partners appears to be the most recommended resilience strategy in the face of current tariff uncertainty.

Implementation: where to start

  1. Supply Chain Audit
    Map the geographical origin of your critical components. Identify dependencies on countries targeted by tariff tensions. The goal is to detect potential breaking points before taxes are applied, by evaluating the weight of customs duties in your current cost structure.
  2. Source diversification
    Do not depend on a single market for your supplies or sales. Develop relationships with suppliers in diversified geographical areas to limit the impact of a targeted trade war. Avoid concentrating your risks in a single unstable political jurisdiction.
  3. Analysis of Non-Tariff Barriers
    Do not focus solely on taxes. Study technical standards, certification requirements, and local regulations that may hinder your exports. Sometimes, the cost of compliance exceeds that of the customs duties themselves.
  4. Managing Adjustment Lags
    Anticipate the delayed effects of tariff policies on inflation and interest rates. Adjust your pricing strategies and financial hedging policies to absorb cost shocks that may occur with a lag of several quarters.
  5. Proactive Strategic Intelligence
    Set up a monitoring unit for trade policy announcements. As the anticipation effect shows, markets react even before taxes come into force. Be prepared to adjust your export or import volumes based on identified political risks.

Indicators to track

  • Evolution of the average cost of imported inputs
  • Dependency rate by geographical sourcing area
  • Average time to comply with non-tariff standards
  • Export volume by target market following tax announcements
  • Variance between forecasted and realized margins following tariff changes

Pitfalls to avoid

  • Ignoring the impact of non-tariff barriers in favor of taxes alone.
  • Underestimating second-round effects on inflation and interest rates.
  • Reacting impulsively without analyzing market adjustment lags.
  • Neglecting the geographical diversification of one's value chain.

For reflection within your organization

  • What share of our revenue is directly exposed to a modification of trade agreements?
  • Are we capable of switching to alternative suppliers in less than six months?
  • Do our current margins allow us to absorb a sudden rise in import costs?

Conclusion

Customs duties are complex instruments whose effects extend far beyond the immediate budgetary framework. For decision-makers, the key lies in operational flexibility and risk diversification. A robust strategy prioritizes cooperation and anticipation rather than mere reaction to protectionist shocks.
