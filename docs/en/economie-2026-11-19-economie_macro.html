Financial inclusion: A lever for economic and social development

Financial inclusion has become a strategic priority for developing nations seeking to reduce poverty. It relies on access to formal banking services, which are now being transformed by technological innovations. This article analyzes the issues, levers, and challenges of this inclusion for decision-makers and economic stakeholders.

Why this topic matters

Financial inclusion refers to the ability of individuals and businesses to access affordable and secure financial products and services tailored to their needs: deposit accounts, credit, payments, or insurance. In developing countries, a large portion of the population remains "unbanked," which limits their ability to save, invest, or cope with economic shocks. This subject is crucial because financial inclusion is not limited to opening an account; it is a driver of macroeconomic growth, inequality reduction, and resilience in the face of crises. By enabling better management of monetary flows, it fosters household empowerment and private sector development. However, access alone is not enough. True inclusion requires solid financial education, robust digital infrastructure, and a protective regulatory framework. Understanding these dynamics is essential for any decision-maker wishing to transform the informal economy into a structured system capable of generating shared and sustainable prosperity for society as a whole.

Key takeaways

To understand financial inclusion, one must distinguish between access and usage. Access is the physical or digital availability of a service, while usage reflects the frequency and relevance of its utilization. Key mechanisms include digital finance (Fintech), which reduces transaction costs and compensates for the lack of physical bank branches. Banking stability is also fundamental: strong banks reinforce public trust, a sine qua non condition for citizens to deposit their savings into the formal system. Two types of exclusion are observed: involuntary exclusion, caused by systemic barriers (excessively heavy administrative requirements, lack of infrastructure), and voluntary exclusion, linked to a lack of trust or financial literacy. Multinational banks play an increasing role by integrating socially sustainable practices, which helps reduce perceived risks. Finally, technological innovation, particularly via mobile money, has radically changed the landscape, making it possible to reach previously isolated geographical areas. The challenge for decision-makers is to create an ecosystem where technology serves people, while protecting consumers against digital risks.

What the studies say

[1] Trends and drivers of inclusion (2010-2023)

This study analyzes 41 developing countries to identify the drivers of financial access. It highlights that inclusion is a major lever for reducing poverty and stabilizing the economy. The results emphasize the need for public policies that combine financial education, technological innovation, and adapted regulatory frameworks. The main limitation lies in the complexity of coordinating these reforms on a large scale to ensure a real impact on the well-being of local communities, which are often held back by persistent structural gaps.

[2] Fintech composite index (2011-2021)

The authors create a new index to measure inclusion via three dimensions: availability, penetration, and usage. Using principal component analysis, they demonstrate a notable progression in mobile and internet usage. While the tool is robust for identifying policy gaps, the study highlights major challenges: the need for massive investment, the crucial importance of customer data protection, and the imperative need to improve financial literacy to support the digital transition.

[3] Role of multinational banks

The study examines 152 multinational banks in 32 countries. It demonstrates that the adoption of socially sustainable practices strengthens the trust of local populations. This virtuous behavior translates concretely into an increase in the demand for bank accounts, a greater propensity to save, and easier access to credit for disadvantaged populations. The main contribution is empirical proof that banking corporate social responsibility (CSR) is not just a marketing concept, but a tangible driver of financial inclusion.

[4] Challenges and systemic barriers

This literature review highlights the gap between technological access and actual usage. It distinguishes between involuntary exclusion (overly strict KYC/AML rules) and voluntary exclusion (lack of trust). The authors conclude that technology alone is not enough: organizational reforms, digital public infrastructure, and flexible regulatory models are essential. The major limitation remains the persistence of cultural and social barriers that hinder adoption, despite the availability of digital tools.

[5] Banking stability and growth

By analyzing 35 countries over the 2011-2022 period, this research establishes a direct link between the stability of the banking sector and economic growth. Stable banks foster economic activity by managing risks better and providing credit. The PCSE method confirms that public trust is the foundation of this dynamic. The study suggests that without regulation guaranteeing the soundness of institutions, financial inclusion efforts risk being futile or unstable.

[6] Islamic finance and Fintech

The bibliometric study (2018-2024) highlights the growing role of Islamic finance in inclusion. Thanks to services compliant with religious principles, these tools attract populations excluded from the conventional system. Dominant themes include P2P lending and blockchain. The conclusion emphasizes that success depends on international collaboration and a strengthening of financial literacy. The limitation is the strong dependence on a specific regulatory framework that still needs to be harmonized to scale up.

Synthesis: convergences, divergences, and uncertainties

Studies converge on a unanimous finding: financial inclusion is an essential driver of development, but it cannot be reduced to a simple technological issue. While Fintech and mobile money are powerful accelerators, their effectiveness is conditioned by three pillars: flexible yet protective regulation, solid banking stability to maintain trust, and increased financial education. Divergences mainly concern the method of evaluating this inclusion, with some researchers favoring composite indices while others focus on specific segments such as Islamic finance or the role of multinationals. An area of uncertainty remains: how to transform digital access into effective and sustainable usage? The gap between owning an account and using it actively remains the major challenge. It is clear that public policies must evolve toward a holistic approach, integrating digital infrastructure, consumer protection, and financial literacy programs, so that inclusion is not an end in itself, but a lever for profound social and economic transformation.

Implementation: where to start

  1. Audit of local needs
    Before deploying solutions, identify the specific obstacles in your area (lack of infrastructure, cultural mistrust, or regulatory barriers). Use field data to understand whether exclusion is voluntary or involuntary. Avoid imposing foreign models without local adaptation.
  2. Strengthening literacy
    Launch simple and accessible financial education programs, particularly via mobile. The goal is to make financial services understandable for populations unfamiliar with the banking system. Do not settle for technical manuals; favor interactive and visual formats.
  3. Adoption of digital finance
    Prioritize interoperable mobile payment solutions. Technology must reduce transaction costs for the end user. Ensure that the platform is intuitive and available in local languages to maximize the adoption rate.
  4. Framework of trust and protection
    Implement strict protocols for data protection and fraud prevention. Trust is fragile; a single security breach can undo years of inclusion efforts. Communicate transparently about user rights.
  5. Multi-stakeholder collaboration
    Unite banks, telecom operators, and regulators to create a coherent ecosystem. Siloing is the main enemy of inclusion. Ensure that KYC (Know Your Customer) policies are proportionate so as not to exclude the most precarious populations.

Indicators to track

  • Bank and mobile account penetration rate
  • Frequency of financial service usage per month
  • Level of financial literacy of target populations
  • Volume of credit granted to small local businesses
  • User satisfaction and trust rate

Pitfalls to avoid

  • Confusing access to services with their effective use by populations.
  • Neglecting financial education in favor of technological innovation alone.
  • Imposing overly rigid compliance rules that exclude the most vulnerable.
  • Underestimating the importance of cultural trust in the banking system.

For reflection within your organization

  • Are our financial services truly adapted to the daily needs of our users?
  • How can we simplify access without compromising data security?
  • What share of our resources is allocated to the educational support of clients?

Conclusion

Financial inclusion is a complex process that requires a long-term vision and close coordination between technology, regulation, and education. By placing people at the center of innovation, policymakers can transform access to banking services into a genuine lever for prosperity. It is time to shift from a logic of simple access to a strategy of sustainable and inclusive usage.
