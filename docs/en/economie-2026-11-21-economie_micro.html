Wages and labor supply: Understanding the new drivers of engagement

The link between compensation and individual effort is no longer just a simple mathematical equation. Between flexibility, subsistence needs, and organizational structures, the dynamics of labor supply are becoming more complex. This article explores the real drivers that motivate individuals to work and how companies can better manage these levers.

Why this topic matters

The question of labor supply—the amount of time an individual is willing to dedicate to paid activity—is at the heart of microeconomics. Traditionally, it relies on a trade-off between leisure and income: the higher the wage, the more the individual is incentivized to work. However, this classic vision is currently being disrupted by the emergence of new forms of employment, such as digital microwork or on-demand service platforms. For a decision-maker, understanding labor supply is crucial for optimizing recruitment, retention, and wage cost management. It is not just about setting a price, but about grasping the deep motivations of workers, whether they are financial or related to flexibility. This article deciphers the mechanisms that influence these decisions, drawing on recent research to move beyond rigid theoretical models and adopt a more pragmatic and human approach to labor relations.

Key takeaways

To properly understand labor supply, one must distinguish between the reservation wage and the effective wage. The reservation wage is the minimum threshold below which an individual refuses to work; it is subjective and depends on personal needs and the value placed on free time. A key concept is the elasticity of labor supply, which measures workers' sensitivity to wage variations. If it is high, a slight increase in compensation attracts many candidates. However, other factors come into play: flexibility, the institutional environment, and social norms. For example, in large companies, wages may be set above the market price to attract top talent or reduce turnover, an approach known as efficiency wage theory. Finally, family constraints, such as parental responsibilities, radically alter workers' availability. Understanding these dynamics allows managers not to limit themselves to a price war, but to build a comprehensive value proposition that resonates with the real expectations of employees.

What the studies say

[1] Motivation in microwork

This study analyzes workers on digital platforms. It shows that while financial motivations and the need for subsistence influence supply, their impact remains limited. The analysis reveals that workers participate for various reasons, ranging from vital necessity to non-essential expenses. The main limitation lies in the very specific nature of these platforms, making generalization to traditional employment complex.

[2] Flexibility and reservation wages

Using data from a ridesharing platform, this research quantifies the value workers place on schedule flexibility. It demonstrates that classic static models underestimate actual reservation wages. The study highlights the importance of using high-frequency dynamic data to avoid observation bias, thus offering a more precise view of the real trade-offs made by modern workers.

[3] Dynamics in Turkey

This analysis covering the 1990-2022 period examines labor supply and demand functions. It concludes that the minimum wage influences corporate demand but does not significantly affect labor supply. The study highlights a structural mismatch where high productivity does not translate into real wage growth, pointing to persistent institutional rigidities.

[4] Fertility and labor supply

This Mexican study shows that the labor supply elasticity of married women varies according to the number of children. After correcting for statistical bias, the results indicate that mothers exhibit higher elasticity than women without children. The major contribution is the demonstration of increasing heterogeneity with family burden, suggesting differentiated wage policies.

[5] Social responsibility and wages

Analyzing global supply chains, this study finds that wages paid often exceed the legal minimum but remain below the subsistence threshold. It demonstrates that trade union rights and freedom of association are the best levers for increasing real wages. The limitation is the difficulty of accessing precise wage data at the supplier level.

[6] Company size and wages

This research challenges the idea that large companies pay more out of market necessity. By studying the German market, it shows that the link between size and salary disappears within the same firm. Large companies appear to pay premiums above the market rate, moving away from classic monopsony models in favor of theories such as efficiency wage theory.

Synthesis: convergences, divergences, and uncertainties

Studies converge on one essential point: salary is not the only determinant of labor supply. Flexibility, union rights, and domestic needs strongly modulate individual decisions. There is a divergence between classic theoretical models, which view the market as an automatic adjustment mechanism, and empirical reality, where companies often set wages above the minimum for strategic reasons (efficiency, retention). What remains uncertain is the ability of institutional structures to adapt quickly to these new realities. As digital work and platforms redefine the notion of availability, public policies and management practices sometimes struggle to keep up. It is clear that the heterogeneity of profiles—particularly regarding family situation or sector—renders uniform salary policies obsolete. Decision-makers must now integrate the value of flexibility and social constraints into their compensation models to remain competitive in a rapidly changing labor market.

Implementation: where to start

  1. Audit the reservation wage
    Conduct internal surveys to understand the real needs of your employees. Do not settle for market averages. Identify what, beyond gross salary, influences their decision to stay or leave, such as schedule flexibility or work-life balance.
  2. Value flexibility
    Offer flexible work options as a lever for non-monetary compensation. Research shows that workers place a high value on control over their schedules. This is a powerful tool for attracting talent without necessarily increasing fixed salary costs.
  3. Segment policies
    Adapt your offers according to the specific needs of your employee segments, particularly parents or precarious workers. A uniform approach is often ineffective. Offer modular benefits that address the real constraints of each demographic group.
  4. Support social dialogue
    Do not view freedom of association as a constraint, but as a means to align wages with a sustainable reality. Harmonious industrial relations foster better productivity and greater staff stability over the long term.
  5. Go beyond the minimum wage
    If you are a large company, adopt an efficiency wage strategy. Paying above the market can reduce recruitment and training costs, while improving the quality of work provided. Avoid rationing labor supply through excessively low wages.

Indicators to track

  • Turnover rate by employee segment
  • Gap between the offered salary and the average reservation wage of candidates
  • Utilization rate of schedule flexibility options
  • Satisfaction index related to the overall compensation structure
  • Evolution of productivity relative to real wage growth

Pitfalls to avoid

  • Believing that salary is the sole lever for worker motivation.
  • Ignoring the specific needs of employees with family responsibilities.
  • Relying solely on global market data without internal analysis.
  • Underestimating the non-monetary value that employees place on flexibility.

For reflection within your organization

  • What monetary value do our employees actually place on the flexibility we offer?
  • Are our salary policies too rigid to meet the heterogeneous needs of our teams?
  • How do our compensation practices influence our ability to retain talent over the long term?

Conclusion

Managing labor supply today requires moving from an accounting-based vision to an approach centered on the human being and their real needs. By integrating flexibility and understanding the deep motivations of your employees, you transform compensation into a true strategic lever. It is time to adjust your policies to reflect the complexity of the current market.
