Customs duties: Understanding the mechanisms and real impacts on global trade

Customs duties have once again become a central lever of national economic policies, directly influencing trade flows and global growth. This article analyzes the complex consequences of these barriers, ranging from industrial protection to disruptions in value chains. Discover how these tools durably influence the strategies of companies and states.

Why this topic matters

In a context of strained globalization, customs duties have become an unavoidable topic for decision-makers. By definition, a customs duty is a tax imposed by a government on imported goods, aimed at protecting domestic industries or correcting trade imbalances. While their stated objective is often to promote local employment and generate tax revenue, their actual effects are far more nuanced. For a manager or a student, understanding this mechanism is essential: it is not merely a tax at the border, but a lever that alters price structures, corporate competitiveness, and diplomatic relations between nations. As geopolitical tensions intensify, the use of tariffs is becoming a strategic negotiation tool. However, this practice carries major risks, particularly the risk of triggering retaliatory measures and curbing long-term economic growth. Analyzing these dynamics allows for better anticipation of market fluctuations and the adjustment of operational strategies accordingly.

Key takeaways

To grasp the effects of customs duties, one must look beyond the simple notion of cost. When a country imposes a tax on an import, it triggers a chain reaction: the price of the good increases for the final consumer, which can reduce demand. At the same time, local companies, protected from foreign competition, may temporarily gain market share. However, this mechanism creates distortions: global supply chains, optimized for efficiency, are disrupted. Importing companies face an increase in their production costs, which reduces their export competitiveness. Furthermore, trading partners often respond with retaliatory measures, creating a protectionist spiral. It is also necessary to distinguish between tariff barriers (direct taxes) and non-tariff barriers, such as technical or sanitary standards, which can be just as restrictive. Finally, economic adjustment lags play a crucial role: inflationary effects and central bank responses, such as interest rate hikes, can cancel out expected benefits by altering investment and consumption behaviors over the long term.

What the studies say

[1] Global impact of tariffs

This study analyzes the impact of tariffs on trade and growth between 1990 and 2020. It demonstrates a significant negative correlation between the average tariff level and GDP growth, particularly for export-dependent economies. While tariffs protect certain sectors, they harm productivity, disrupt value chains, and limit access to technologies. The main limitation lies in the fact that, although strategic, these measures hinder liberalization and long-term economic development.

[2] Dynamic modeling of tariffs

The study uses delay differential equations to model tariffs as a feedback mechanism. It highlights that the effects are not immediate: tariffs cause inflationary pressures that push central banks to adjust interest rates, thereby impacting investment and consumption. The research highlights the crucial role of economic adjustment lags, allowing decision-makers to anticipate the indirect and delayed consequences of trade policies on macroeconomic stability.

[3] India-USA agricultural trade

Using a gravity model, this research examines tariff and non-tariff barriers between India and the United States. The results indicate that the elimination of customs duties would significantly boost U.S. exports to India, particularly for fresh fruits and nuts, while reducing prices for Indian consumers. The study highlights the potential for bilateral growth if restrictions are lifted, although it focuses on a specific sector, limiting generalization to the entire economy.

[4] Korea-EU agreement and non-tariffs

This analysis breaks down the effects of the Korea-EU free trade agreement. Contrary to expectations, Korean exports fell by 17.6% despite the reduction in tariffs. The study shows that tariff gains were canceled out by non-tariff barriers, particularly in the manufacturing sector. The value of this research lies in isolating non-tariff effects, revealing that the reduction of customs duties does not automatically guarantee an increase in trade if other obstacles persist.

[5] Trade wars and the long term

This study examines the lasting consequences of trade wars, such as the one between the USA and China. It identifies persistent effects such as financial market volatility, supply chain disruption, and profound sectoral impacts. The author concludes that while national measures may offer immediate political gains, global prosperity requires stable cooperation. The limitation is the uncertainty linked to the diversification of trading partners as a survival strategy.

[6] Effects of Trump 2.0 tariffs

This ex-post study analyzes data from 31 countries following the tariffs imposed in 2025. It observes an anticipation effect with a rise in exports just before they came into effect, followed by a sharp drop afterward. Countries redirected their flows to smaller economies to compensate. The study highlights the responsiveness of companies to political announcements, although the analysis is limited to a very short period following the implementation of the measures.

Synthesis: convergences, divergences, and uncertainties

Studies converge on one major point: while tariffs may serve short-term political objectives, they are generally detrimental to long-term economic growth. The main divergence lies in the complexity of the effects: while some view tariffs as a protectionist tool, others demonstrate that non-tariff barriers and chain reactions (inflation, interest rates, retaliatory measures) often cancel out expected benefits. It remains uncertain to what extent companies can truly diversify their supply chains to counter these shocks without suffering major productivity losses. Data also show that effects are heterogeneous across sectors and countries, making any tariff policy difficult to calibrate with precision. In short, the literature emphasizes that the stability of international relations and the reduction of non-tariff barriers are more effective levers for prosperity than the use of customs duties, whose side effects are often underestimated by policymakers.

Implementation: where to start

  1. Dependency audit
    Identify the share of your inputs subject to customs duties or non-tariff barriers. Analyze the vulnerability of your supply chain to a sudden increase in taxes. The goal is to map your risks to avoid stock shortages or unforeseen cost price explosions.
  2. Geographic diversification
    Do not depend on a single trading partner or a single geographic area. Explore alternative free trade agreements to source your components. Diversification allows you to absorb tariff shocks imposed by a specific country by shifting your flows to more competitive or less restrictive zones.
  3. Monitoring non-tariff measures
    Monitor changes in standards, certifications, or technical regulations. Often more discreet than tariffs, these barriers can block your exports despite reduced customs duties. Invest in regulatory compliance to maintain your access to foreign markets.
  4. Anticipating lead times
    Integrate economic reaction times (inflation, interest rates) into your financial models. Do not make decisions based on immediate prices, as tariff policies cause delayed adjustments. Provide leeway to absorb currency fluctuations and central bank responses.
  5. Competitiveness analysis
    Regularly evaluate your price competitiveness against local competition in your target markets. If tariffs increase, calculate whether a strategy of moving upmarket or differentiation can offset the rise in the final price for the consumer, rather than suffering a loss of market share.

Indicators to track

  • Import dependency rate by geographic area
  • Evolution of unit cost prices post-tariffs
  • Average time to comply with non-tariff barriers
  • Share of exports redirected to alternative markets
  • Correlation between tariff variations and net margins

Pitfalls to avoid

  • Ignoring non-tariff barriers in favor of analyzing only customs duties.
  • Underestimating the retaliatory effects of trading partners on your own exports.
  • Believing that the effects of a tariff policy are immediate and without inflationary consequences.
  • Neglecting the impact of interest rates and monetary policies on your financing costs.

For reflection within your organization

  • What is our actual capacity to absorb a 10% increase in import costs?
  • Are we too dependent on a single market for our exports?
  • How can our internal processes adapt more quickly to new international technical standards?

Conclusion

Customs duties are complex tools whose effects extend far beyond the immediate budgetary framework. Prudent management requires a holistic vision, integrating economic adjustment timelines and non-tariff barriers. Ultimately, corporate resilience will depend on the ability to diversify partnerships and anticipate global macroeconomic dynamics.
