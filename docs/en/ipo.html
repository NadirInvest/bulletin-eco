Investor Guide

What is an IPO?

An Initial Public Offering (IPO) is the first sale of a company's shares to the public on a stock exchange. The company generally seeks to raise capital for growth, provide liquidity to its shareholders, increase its profile, or allow early investors to exit. Once listed, it must regularly publish financial information.

Main types of operations

• Initial Public Offering (IPO): offering of shares to the public, with or without the issuance of new shares. • Direct listing: listing of existing shares, without a new offering or capital raising. • Capital increase with preferential subscription rights: existing shareholders have priority to subscribe to new shares, which limits the dilution of their stake (depending on the rules of each country). • Capital increase without preferential rights (private or accelerated placement): new shares offered to selected investors, often institutional. • Secondary offering: existing shareholders sell their shares; the company does not receive the proceeds. • Bond issuance or convertible bonds: debt that can be converted into shares.

Typical process of an operation

1. The company chooses its advisory banks and prepares its audited accounts. 2. A draft prospectus is filed with the market regulator. 3. The regulator reviews the document and then approves it or grants its visa, depending on the country. 4. A price range is announced, then investors express their demand (bookbuilding). 5. The final price is set and shares are allocated; the allocation may be less than the demand. 6. Trading of the shares begins. Existing shareholders are often subject to a lock-up period for their securities.

How to read a prospectus

The prospectus is the official document for the operation. Focus on: the summary of the operation; risk factors; intended use of the funds raised; historical accounts and their trends; shareholding before and after the operation (measuring dilution); governance and executive compensation; potential conflicts of interest; dividend policy; terms of the offer (price or range, schedule, allocation rules).

How to participate in an operation

Subscription is carried out through an authorized intermediary (bank or broker) in the country of the relevant stock exchange. Some transactions are reserved for institutional investors; others are open to the public with a subscription period and allocation rules. Before investing abroad, check: exchange rate rules, taxation of dividends and capital gains, withholding taxes and tax treaties, fees, and the protection offered by the local regulator.

Risks to keep in mind

• High price volatility, especially in the first few months. • An issue price that may be too high or too low relative to the company's actual value. • Limited trading history and sometimes recent or high-growth financial statements that are difficult to compare. • Dilution during a capital increase if you do not subscribe. • Liquidity, exchange rate, and country risk for foreign markets. • A controlling shareholder who may retain most of the power. Past performance is not indicative of future results.

Ten questions to ask yourself before subscribing

1. What does the company do, and how does it make money? 2. What will the funds raised be used for? 3. Are the financial statements audited and consistent over several years? 4. What is the implicit valuation compared to similar companies? 5. Who are the shareholders before and after the transaction? 6. What dilution does the transaction entail? 7. What are the three main risks cited by the company? 8. What is the lock-up period for existing shareholders? 9. What are the fees, taxes, and exchange constraints for me? 10. What portion of my savings am I prepared to expose to this transaction?
